Elena grabbed the trackpad, her fingers cold against the plastic. The console did not ask for a password; the terminal was left wide open, its diagnostic utility running in an active loop. She clicked into the top-level directory labeled PROJ_HADIS. Dozens of encrypted datablocks were locked behind red security icons, but an unencrypted log partition sat in a staging folder labeled FIELD_TRANSCRIPT_ARCHIVE. Elena pulled up the most recent entry, dated three days prior. It wasn't a typed report. It was an automated audio transcription log, synchronized with acoustic sensors from the lower hold: [02:14:19 UTC] Dr. Sorenson (Lead Biologist): "The core temperature within Module 36 is dropping faster than the chillers can account for. It’s drawing thermal energy directly out of the ambient brine." [02:15:02 UTC] Chief Engineer Vance: "I told you, the ship's auxiliary generators can't sustain that inductive load. The bus bars are melting in tunnel three. If you don't vent the coolant ring, we lose main propulsion." [02:15:40 UTC] Dr. Sorenson: "Do not touch the venting valves. If the pressure falls below sixty atmospheres, the metabolic boundary breaks down. We pulled it from eight thousand meters in the Molloy Deep—it doesn't have an anatomy as you understand it. It is an endolithic colonial mass. It metabolizes pressure gradients." [02:16:11 UTC] Chief Engineer Vance: "Doc, look at the sensor. It’s not metabolizing the gradient. It’s expanding into it." [02:17:05 UTC] [ACOUSTIC SPIKE: 142 dB] [02:17:12 UTC] Dr. Sorenson: "Seal the hatch. Seal the hatch right now—" [02:17:30 UTC] [TRANSCRIPTION TERMINATED: SIGNAL DEGRADATION] Elena’s pulse thudded in her temples. The Molloy Deep—the deepest oceanic trench in the Arctic Basin, a pitch-black abyssal trench miles beneath the ice pack. Whatever Vanguard Bio-Geophysical had dredged from the seabed floor wasn't cargo. It was a deep-sea benthic specimen that required crushing abyssal pressure to remain inert. And they had brought it to the surface in an insulated cryogenic shell. A sudden shudder ripped through the Orion Trader. The deck plates beneath Elena’s boots dropped an inch, then snapped upward as a deafening hydraulic shriek echoed through the bulkheads. Across the laptop display, error lines cascaded in fluorescent red: MODULE 36: HYDRAULIC ANCHOR SHEARED HOLD 4 STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY: 8% SEAWATER INTRUSION DETECTED: DOUBLE BOTTOM VOID 3 BALLAST TANK 4 STARBOARD: FLOODING The ship rolled sharply to starboard. In the electrical closet, a rack of spare fuses slid off the shelf, shattering across the steel deck. The creature—or whatever colonial organism was waking up—was tearing through the internal ballast framing, venting thousands of gallons of sea water into the hold to reconstruct its native pressure environment. The freighter was taking on water from the inside out, listing hard to the right. Elena didn't hesitate. She jammed a thumb drive from her pocket into the laptop's USB port, slammed the key combination to clone the active directory, and watched the progress bar crawl to 100%. She ripped the drive out, shoved it deep into her immersion suit pocket, and lunged for the doorway. She had to get off this ship now. She burst into the machinery corridor. The ambient air was no longer just warm—it was thick, humid, and smelled rancid, like rotting kelp, sulfur, and burning electrical insulation. As she reached the base of the stairwell leading up toward the weather deck, she glanced down the companionway toward the welded door of Hold 4. The heavy four-foot rebar was bowed outward like a drawn archer's bow. From the seams around the watertight seal, thick, viscous white foam was bubbling through the gap, hissing as it made contact with the hot deck plating. The chalky dendritic crust wasn't mineral—it was fibrous, expanding, pushing tendrils through the microscopic fractures of the rubber gasket. Then came the knock again. Not three taps this time. A single, cataclysmic slam that dented the steel plate outward, popping three structural rivets across the frame like rifle shots. Elena turned and bolted up the stairs. She emerged through the deckhouse door onto the main deck into absolute chaos. The squall had broken in full fury. Driving sheets of freezing Atlantic rain whipped across the container stacks, turning the ocean into a churning grey nightmare. The Orion Trader was listing a noticeable ten degrees to starboard, its bow dipping unsteadily into the cresting whitecaps. Elena reached the starboard railing and wiped the stinging brine from her eyes, looking down into the trough of the waves. The pilot boat Gannet was gone. Fifty yards off the freighter’s quarter, she caught a glimpse of the Gannet’s orange wheelhouse riding high on a rolling swell. Benny was fighting the helm, reversing engines to avoid being crushed against the freighter's rolling hull. Elena reached for her VHF, remembering instantly that the radio was dead. She unclipped her high-intensity emergency strobe, raised it above her head, and switched it to rapid pulse. Across the water, the searchlight on the Gannet flickered on, cutting through the driving rain. The light swept the container rail, locking squarely onto Elena. A horn blasted twice from the pilot boat: a signal that Benny saw her, but couldn't come alongside the lee rail without being sucked under the freighter's hull or smashed against the bilge keel. Then, behind Elena, a massive groaning rip tore through the center of the cargo deck. Forty feet away, the heavy steel pontoon hatch of Hold 4—weighing over thirty tons—buckled upward, its industrial locking pins snapping with the sound of cracking artillery. A geyser of black, freezing ballast water erupted into the rain, carrying with it a cloud of pale, fibrous vapor that rolled outward across the stacks. Do you want to head for the lifeboat station on the boat deck, attempt a high-risk leap into the sea toward the pilot boat, or race to the bridge to drop the emergency sea anchors and stabilize the list?
