The North Atlantic swell shoved the pilot boat hard against the black wall of the freighter’s hull, the rubber fenders groaning in protest. Above them, the ship rose six stories out of the grey water, its name painted in weathered white block letters: ORION TRADER. Elena Reyes adjusted the strap of her waterproof gear bag and waited for the swell to peak before grabbing the rusted steel rungs of the pilot ladder. Below her, the deckhand held the throttle steady against the chop. "Three hours until the squall hits, Inspector," he shouted over the wind. "If you’re not back on the rail by fourteen hundred, I’m pulling off. I won't lose my boat against her flank." "Understood," Elena called back. She climbed without looking down, her boots finding traction on the slick salt-crust until her hands gripped the bulwark and she vaulted onto the main deck. Silence settled over her immediately. It was not the quiet of an abandoned dock, but the muffled, unnatural hush of an active vessel running on autopilot. Deep beneath the steel plates, the steady, rhythmic pulse of the low-speed diesel engine vibrated against the soles of her boots. The ship was alive, yet entirely still. Elena pulled her handheld VHF radio from its holster. "Bridge, this is Maritime Safety Inspector Elena Reyes, salvage authority boarder. Does anyone copy?" Static hissed in reply.

She moved along the starboard gangway toward the superstructure. The Orion Trader was a standard 600-foot container carrier, en route from Rotterdam to Halifax. Thirty-six hours ago, its automated transponder had ceased reporting position data, drifting fifty miles south of the commercial shipping lanes. Coast Guard radar had picked it up six hours ago, drifting at a lazy three knots with zero response to radio hailing. Reaching the weather-tight door to the deckhouse, Elena reached for the dogging lever. It was unlatched. She pulled the heavy steel door open and stepped inside, letting it seal shut behind her. The corridor smelled of ozone, burnt toast, and damp floor mats. She took the stairs two at a time, moving past the crew mess. On the long communal table, lunch remained half-eaten. Three bowls of stew sat cold under a film of grease; a metal spoon rested balanced on the rim of a ceramic mug. There was no sign of a struggle—no overturned chairs, no shattered glass, no blood. It looked as though the crew had simply stood up mid-sentence and vanished into thin air. Elena climbed the central stairwell until she reached the navigation bridge.

The bridge was panoramic and bright under the overcast sky. Green and amber status lights blinked in rhythmic patterns across the primary consoles. The radar sweep rotated faithfully, painting a clean circle of empty sea. On the captain’s console, a ceramic mug held dark coffee, still lukewarm to the touch. Elena stepped over to the ship’s logbook, open flat beside the helm. The handwriting in blue ballpoint was neat and measured. The last entry had been logged yesterday at 04:15 UTC: Course 272. Speed 14 knots. Calm seas. Engine room reports intermittent acoustic anomalies on hydrophone feed. Chief engineer descending to lower ballast tunnel to inspect transducer casing. Nothing followed. The rest of the page was clean.

Elena turned toward the radio console. The main transmitter was set to marine channel 16, fully powered, volume at halfway. The emergency position-indicating beacon sat undisturbed in its yellow cradle on the bulkhead. The lifeboats outside hung perfectly secured in their davits. A sharp mechanical click echoed from the far corner of the bridge. Elena froze, one hand dropping instinctively to the heavy flashlight at her hip. The sound came from the automated printer hooked to the vessel’s internal diagnostic system. The paper feed jerked forward, spewing out three inches of fresh thermal paper. She walked over and tore the strip away. Printed in standard monospace type was a single line:

HOLD 4: PRESSURE EQUALIZATION COMPLETE. CARGO MANIFEST MISMATCH.

Below it, the system timestamp was marked just three minutes ago.

Elena looked through the forward bridge windows down at the endless rows of colorful shipping containers lashed to the foredeck. Somewhere far below deck, beneath thousands of tons of locked steel, a heavy, dull thud reverberated through the hull.
