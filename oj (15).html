The impact was an iron fist against Elena’s skull. The lifeboat slammed into the Atlantic trough at thirty knots, plunging deep beneath the boiling foam. The fiberglass hull shrieked under the sudden hydrodynamic pressure, the interior lights flickering out before snapping back to a dull, battery-powered emergency amber. Cold seawater exploded through the perimeter vents, dousing the cabin in freezing spray before the automatic buoyancy chambers kicked in, rocketing the craft back to the surface. The pod breached like a cork, rolling violently forty degrees to port before self-righting in the crest of a mountainous swell. Inside, Thorne had not managed to buckle into a safety seat. He lay crumpled against the aft bulkhead, clutching his right shoulder where the collarbone had visibly snapped on impact. Beside him, sliding across the wet non-skid floor with every heave of the sea, was the stainless-steel cryo-canister. Elena ripped off her four-point harness, her head spinning with nausea. She lunged into the coxswain's seat, wiped the saltwater mist from the forward viewport, and looked out. Through the heavy rain, the final moments of the Orion Trader unfolded in horrific grandeur. The freighter had split completely in two. The forward half—Hold 4 and the container stacks—was already nearly submerged, dragging hundreds of tons of colorful steel boxes down into the dark. But it wasn't sinking naturally under gravity. It was being pulled. The colossal benthic matriarch was wrapped around the severed hull like an ivory vice, its pulsing mantle displacing walls of white foam. Huge geysers of pressurized air and pulverized rust erupted from the broken vents as the entity dragged the wreckage straight down toward the abyssal trench. A hundred yards to the north, the research vessel Aegir was burning. Its sleek bridge superstructure had collapsed under the weight of several crystalline spires, its radar masts snapped like dry toothpicks. Dead in the water, listing heavily to its starboard rail, the ship was being systematically engulfed by sheets of pale, fibrous foam that smothered its deck fires in clouds of choking white vapor. The two vessels that had disturbed the deep-trench specimen were being scrubbed from the surface of the earth. A metallic scrape tore Elena’s attention back into the lifeboat. Thorne was on his knees. Using his uninjured left arm, he dragged the cryo-canister toward himself, cradling it against his ribs like an infant. His face was beaded with cold sweat, his breathing ragged, shallow gasps. "You don't understand what this is, Reyes," he rasped, his eyes glassy and feverish in the amber emergency light. "The bio-electric density... the endolithic matrix... it doesn't decay. It reorganizes. It processes data thousands of times faster than silicon, surviving in environments that would vaporize standard biology. Vanguard's backers spent twelve years tracing the tectonic anomalies to find that trench. We hold the patent to the next evolutionary baseline." "Look at yourself, Thorne," Elena said, stepping carefully down the center aisle, her one bare wool sock squelching on the deck. "Look at the seal on that cylinder." Thorne froze. He looked down at the heavy lid of the container. The digital display on the canister’s vacuum gauge was flashing red: INTERNAL TEMP: +12°C PRESSURE: 1.2 ATM CONTAINMENT INTEGRITY: COMPROMISED The violent jolt of the free-fall drop had cracked the primary seal. From the micro-fracture along the stainless-steel rim, a fine, chalky vapor was hissing into the cabin. And creeping across the metal surface, white and delicate as frost on a morning windowpane, were tiny dendritic tendrils. They weren't inert. The hair-like filaments twitched, smelling the warm, carbon-rich air of the sealed lifeboat cabin—and the fresh blood dripping from Thorne’s broken shoulder.
