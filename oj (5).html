The geyser of black ballast water rained down over the container bays, slicking the steel deck with a pungent, mineral-dense slush. Elena didn't look back at the buckled hatch. Jumping into a churning North Atlantic swell with a ten-degree list was suicide—the Orion Trader’s hull was acting like a massive vertical wall, creating a deadly suction trough that would drag her under the bilge keel before the Gannet could ever maneuver close enough to fish her out. Her only viable exit was the starboard enclosed lifeboat on the boat deck, two levels above the main deck. She sprinted along the narrow gangway, hauling herself up the exterior steel companionway. Wind tore at her hood, driving sleet against her skin. Behind her, deep within the yawning breach of Hold 4, the sound was no longer hydraulic—it was biological. A wet, tearing rasp, like canvas ripping under immense tension, accompanied by the groaning twist of six-inch steel girders being bent out of true. She reached the boat deck. The orange fiberglass survival craft hung securely in its gravity davits, rigged for immediate free-fall or winch deployment. Elena scrambled toward the boarding hatch, her fingers cold and stiff as she reached for the release lever. The mechanism was jammed. She leaned her entire body weight against the primary dogging lever, but it refused to budge. Wiping rain from her eyes, she leaned closer. Inside the recessed locking pin housing, thick, pale calcifications had already begun to set—the same chalky, fibrous dendritic growth she had seen on the inspection port below. It had traveled up through the vessel's internal gray-water lines and scuppers, crystallizing upon contact with the open air. The release cable was frozen solid inside a sheath of brittle white crust. A low, percussive vibration traveled through the superstructure, so violent it rattled the fillings in her teeth. Elena whirled around. At the forward edge of the boat deck, peering down over the container stacks, she saw it. Rising from the cavernous opening of Hold 4 was not a creature with eyes, jaws, or limbs. It was an undulating, pale mass of semi-translucent tissue, shifting between dense fibrous muscle and crystalline lattice. It expanded rapidly as the ambient atmospheric pressure dropped, billowing upward like an enormous, slow-motion eruption of subterranean coral. As it breached the open air, long, pale tendrils anchored themselves into the container lashings, splintering inch-thick steel cables with effortless leverage. The ship lurched again, listing sharply to fifteen degrees. The Gannet was retreating into the squall line, its horn sounding one final, desperate blast before disappearing behind a wall of grey sea. Elena was completely cut off. The only space on the vessel with independent power, satellite communication reroutes, and manual override to the emergency sea anchors was the bridge. If she could lock down the bridge bulkheads and drop the port-side sea anchors, she might stabilize the list and buy enough time to transmit a distress burst on the military emergency channel before the organism capsized the entire ship. She bolted for the bridge access door, throwing her shoulder against the dogging handle. She slipped inside, slammed the heavy steel door, and drove the manual deadbolts home. For three seconds, the only sound was her ragged breathing and the howl of the storm against the reinforced glass. Then, across the bridge console, every single primary screen flickered violently from green to blinding white. A distorted, mechanical voice hummed to life from the bridge intercom speakers—slow, stuttered, and assembling words from pre-recorded system alerts: "P-pressure... equal... ized. Trajectory... locked. Welcome... aboard." The helm wheel began to spin on its own.
