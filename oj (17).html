The axe head struck the brass shear-pin of the aft hatch with a concussive chime. The mechanism didn't budge. Elena adjusted her footing on the wet diamond-plate floor, raised the heavy axe again, and brought the tempered steel down in a brutal, two-handed swing against the release dog. Crack. The dogging arm snapped free, and the pressurized Atlantic storm did the rest. The heavy oval hatch blew outward on its hydraulic hinges. Instantly, the cabin was swallowed by deafening noise. A torrent of sub-zero sea air and freezing brine roared inside, immediately cutting through the stifling, ozone-heavy humidity of the interior. The thermal shock was violent and immediate. The expanding biomass across the floor plates shrieked—a sound like dry ice tossed into boiling water. Its translucent, gelatinous membrane clouded into a dull, chalky white, the moisture inside flash-crystallizing into brittle lattice under the blast of freezing salt spray. The cerulean glow pulsed erratically, dimming from vibrant neon to a dying ember. On the deck, Thorne’s half-mineralized body shuddered. The tendrils wrapped around his torso snapped like dry icicles as he pitched sideways toward the open threshold. The creature inside him was desperate, recoiling from the freezing atmospheric air, trying to anchor into the warm wiring loom beneath the coxswain's seat. Elena didn't let it anchor. She reversed her grip on the axe, drove the pick end into the outer flange of the stainless-steel cylinder, and shoved with all her weight. The cylinder, along with the core mass clinging to its rim, skittered across the slick floorboards. It hit the combing of the open doorway, tipped over the lip, and tumbled into the roaring, pitch-black Atlantic trough thirty feet below. Behind it, Thorne’s petrified frame slid into the wash of incoming water. His fingers, encrusted in white mineral bloom, scraped futilely against the non-skid deck before the sea dragged him out into the churning wake. Elena lunged for the door's manual dogging chain. Fighting against the howling updraft of the waves, she hauled the heavy fiberglass door shut and slammed the lower latch into place. The latch clicked. The silence that followed was relative, but immense. Inside the lifeboat, the air was freezing, reeking of brine, spent ozone, and pulverized mineral dust. Across the cabin floor, the remaining tendrils had withered into inert, greyish-white ash, blowing about in the draft from the perimeter vents. Elena slumped against the sealed hatch, her breath coming in jagged white plumes. The axe slipped from her numb fingers, clattering onto the deck. She pulled herself up and limped to the forward console. The digital displays were dead, the primary navigation system fried by the entity’s bio-electric discharge. But mounted above the windscreen, wired directly to an independent dry-cell battery, sat the vessel’s analog magnetic compass and the mechanical emergency locator switch. The compass needle spun erratically for a moment, settling due north. Elena flipped the plastic guard off the emergency switch and toggled the brass lever to TRANSMIT. High above the fiberglass canopy, the EPIRB beacon pulsed a single, rhythmic flash of amber light into the storm.
