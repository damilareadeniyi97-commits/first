The air in the wheelhouse plummeted toward zero, frost blooming in jagged fractals across the glass displays. Elena did not scream. The instinct was swallowed by the sudden, razor-sharp clarity of survival. The thing that had been Chief Engineer Vance lurched forward. Its movements were sickeningly mechanical—not the stagger of a corpse, but the jerky calibration of a marionette whose strings were being shortened and pulled by an unseen hand. From the cuffs of the yellow slicker, glistening white tendrils reached toward the bulkheads, finding seams, cable raceways, and exposed bolts, anchoring the dead weight against the violent roll of the deck. The organism wasn't reanimating Vance; it was wearing him. It was using his calcified nervous system and motor memory to interact with the ship’s controls. Vance’s arm rose, the pale, crust-covered fingers reaching directly for the master engine telegraph to increase speed. Elena dropped her shoulder, threw her entire weight into the red manual trip-lever, and yanked it down. Clack-bang. A dull explosion rumbled from the foredeck as the pyrotechnic bolt severed the anchor brake. Outside, in the freezing sleet, five hundred feet of heavy forged-iron chain tore free from the port hawsepipe, shrieking like a dying leviathan as tons of steel spilled into the open ocean. The sudden drag of the hanging chain bit hard into the Atlantic swell. The Orion Trader shuddered violently, groaning as its hull fought the immense downward and aftward tension. The bow swung hard to port, counteracting the automated rudder. The violent yaw hurled Vance’s reanimated frame sideways. The corpse slammed into the radar pedestal, several brittle, chalky tendrils snapping with the sound of dry pine branches. A wet, gasping hiss erupted from the figure’s calcified chest—a release of pressurized organic vapor that smelled of sulfur and deep-sea brine. Elena didn't wait for it to recover. She scooped up the heavy brass parallel ruler from the chart table, vaulted over the central console, and drove the heavy edge of the tool directly into the creature's knee joint. The leg buckled with a dry crunch. The thing toppled backward onto the linoleum, fibrous tendrils whipping blindly through the air like severed electrical wires, seeking purchase. Elena bolted for the heavy exterior door leading out to the bridge wing. She slammed her palms against the emergency push-bar. The door flew open into the howling gale, the freezing draft catching the door and smashing it against the outer bulkhead. She stepped out onto the metal grating of the bridge wing, sixty feet above the surging ocean. The cold was suffocating, but below her, the deck was changing. The dragging anchor chain had slowed the vessel’s turn, but it hadn’t stopped the expansion. Down in the cargo well, Hold 4 was no longer just venting water—it was erupting. Huge, pulsing spires of semi-translucent, milky-white biomass were bridging the gaps between shipping containers, fusing them into a solid, jagged monolith. The containers were crumpling inward like empty soda cans, crushed by the contractile force of the organism as it pulled materials into its core to reinforce its bulk against the low atmospheric pressure of the surface. And then, looking toward the stern, Elena saw something that made her breath freeze in her throat. Rising from the ventilation exhaust vents above the engine room were dozens of slender, bioluminescent stalks, each glowing with an unnatural, sickly cerulean light. They swayed against the gale, rhythmic and synchronized, tuned to an acoustic frequency she couldn't hear, but could feel vibrating behind her eyes. A massive shudder ran through the entire length of the hull. The engine didn't die. Instead, the steady thrum of the diesel changed pitch, dropping into a deep, groaning resonance that bypassed the exhaust pipes and vibrated directly into the surrounding water. It was broadcasting a beacon. A low-frequency biological sonar pulse, projected miles out into the abyssal trenches of the North Atlantic. Inside the bridge, the radio on the navigation console sparked back to life with a blast of deafening static. A voice cut through the squall—crackling, intermittent, but unmistakably human: "Vessel calling... emergency frequency... this is the research vessel Aegir... we have your transponder signature. Do not attempt... repeat, DO NOT vent Hold 4..." The transmission broke into static, followed by a cold, clear sentence: "We are coming alongside to secure the specimen."
