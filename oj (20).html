The quarantine ward at Shearwater was a concrete cube stripped of personality, smelling of industrial bleach and sterile paper. For thirty-six hours, Elena remained under constant surveillance. Medical personnel in positive-pressure suits drew blood, ran mass-spectrometry scans, and scraped skin samples from her frostbitten foot. Outside the reinforced door, two federal guards stood round-the-clock watch. Miller returned on the second morning, carrying an empty steel evidence case. "Your biological panels are clean, Inspector," he said, setting the case on the stainless-steel table. "No trace of anomalous compounds. No parasitic markers. You’re being discharged to civilian medical in Halifax." He paused, his gaze boring into hers. "We’ve reviewed the satellite thermal logs. The Orion Trader and the Aegir both cleared the continental shelf before foundering. The official incident report will cite catastrophic structural failure due to unpredicted rogue-wave impacts in heavy sea-state six. Vanguard has already initiated hull-loss insurance claims." "A rogue wave," Elena repeated flatly. "And the twenty-two men?" "Lost at sea. Unrecoverable." Miller leaned in, his voice dropping to a low, warning hum. "Vanguard Bio-Geophysical dissolved its corporate charter twelve hours ago. It no longer exists. There is no entity to sue, no board to subpoena, and no salvage to claim. The Atlantic is six thousand meters deep out there, Inspector. Let it stay at the bottom." Elena looked down at her hands, bandage-wrapped and scabbed from the freezing salt air. "Of course, Agent Miller. A rogue wave." Two hours later, she was wheeled out through the rear civilian exit of the naval station. Instead of waiting for the government liaison car, Elena paid cash for a battered city cab and directed the driver to a small commercial internet café nestled in the industrial waterfront of Halifax. The room was dim, quiet, and occupied only by two offshore deckhands nursing cheap coffees. Elena chose a terminal in the far corner, away from the street-facing windows. She pulled the small pocketknife from her boot kit, slit the inner nylon lining of her gear vest, and drew out the black thumb drive. She plugged it into the terminal. The screen blinked. The drive’s directory mounted without error: four gigabytes of raw acoustic data, seismic telemetry from the Molloy Deep, the encrypted communication logs of Dr. Thorne, and the final audio transcript of the bridge crew being consumed by the entity in Hold 4. Elena didn't upload the files to a single regulatory agency or government server. Instead, she initiated a multi-node, peer-to-peer torrent protocol, mirroring the unencrypted raw data across three public academic archives: the International Maritime Organization's accident clearinghouse, an independent marine biology open-science repository in Oslo, and six international investigative journalism syndicates. She opened the transmission window and typed a single header note: Vessel: Orion Trader. Incident: Project Hadis. Location: Molloy Deep / North Atlantic Basin. The wreckage is dead, but the parent mass remains active. Do not dredge the trench. She clicked BROADCAST. The progress bar leaped from zero to one hundred percent in seconds. Across the globe, thousands of decentralized nodes verified and mirrored the data. Elena pulled the drive from the port, dropped it onto the floor beneath her heel, and crushed the silicon wafer into fine black plastic dust. She stood up, pulling her wool coat tightly around her shoulders, and walked out into the chilly Halifax morning. The harbor stretched out before her—grey, vast, and cold under the pale autumn sun. Out beyond the breakwater, the North Atlantic rolled toward the horizon, dark and fathomless, holding its secrets beneath miles of crushing water. For the first time in three days, the deep, rhythmic thrum in her ears was gone. There was only the sound of gulls crying over the tide, and the cold wind sweeping clean across the sea.
