The helm wheel spun violently to starboard, clicking through its hydraulic stops before locking at full rudder. Through the bridge’s forward panoramic glass, the horizon tilted at a dizzying angle. The Orion Trader was carving a hard, shuddering turn directly into the teeth of the gale. The sudden lateral torque threw Elena sideways; her hip slammed against the radar pedestal, knocking the breath from her lungs. She dragged herself upright, clutching the edge of the navigation console. The ship’s automated heading indicator was spinning down through degree marks—272... 240... 190... 165—stabilizing on a heading due south-southeast. Not toward Halifax. Not toward any commercial lane or coastline. It was steering straight into the deep Atlantic basin, aimed directly at the oceanic fracture zones. The synthesized voice had not come from the standard automated alert bank. It was spliced together from fragments of the ship’s digitized training prompts, distorted by a low-frequency hum that vibrated the bridge's display casings. On the central navigational chart, the route line had been rewritten. The destination waypoint was no longer marked with a harbor coordinate; it was locked to a depth coordinate: 6,200 METERS. The organism wasn't just expanding into the void of Hold 4. It was infiltrating the ship's fly-by-wire steering actuators through the lower cable trays. Elena lunged for the helm console, flipped the clear plastic cover off the manual steering bypass, and slapped the emergency override switch. Clack. Nothing happened. The digital status light remained frozen on REMOTE / SLAVE PROTOCOL ACTIVE. A series of rapid mechanical pops echoed from the ceiling vents. Elena aimed her flashlight upward. Pushing through the aluminum louvers were thin, glistening filaments—translucent as fishing line, but moving with deliberate, contractile pulses. They weren't roots; they were biological conduits, searching for electrical leads. The moment a filament brushed against the exposed copper ground of the overhead lighting panel, a sharp arc of blue sparks snapped, followed by the pungent stench of singed organic matter. The filaments didn't retreat. They twitched, stiffened, and began drawing current directly from the fixture, glowing with a faint, bioluminescent pale blue. Elena scrambled behind the main console to the emergency drop panel for the ground tackle. The port and starboard anchors were rigged with explosive guillotine releases—purely mechanical, gravity-fed, intended to arrest runaway drift in shallow waters or stabilize a catastrophic roll. Dropping the port anchor in deep water wouldn't hit bottom, but hundreds of fathoms of heavy forged chain plummeting into the trough would act as a massive sea brake, violently jerking the ship’s head back to port and interrupting the automated turn. She gripped the red mechanical trip-lever labeled PORT ANCHOR WINCH BRAKE: MANUAL RELEASE. A cold draft washed over the back of her neck. Elena paused, every instinct screaming. The air in the bridge was dropping in temperature at an impossible rate. Her breath bloomed in pale plumes before her face. Frost—real, crystalline frost—was creeping rapidly across the chart table, tracing along the contours of the brass dividers and paper manifests. She turned around slowly. Standing in the companionway leading down to the captain’s quarters was a figure clad in a yellow foul-weather slicker. The coat was stiff with frozen brine, the sleeves shredded at the cuffs. The face beneath the hood was obscured by a thick, calcified mask of pale, fibrous growth that had grown outward from the nasal cavity and mouth, sealing the jaw into a permanent, silent grimace. The badge clipped to the chest read: VANCE, E. — CHIEF ENGINEER. The corpse took a stiff, uncoordinated step forward. Its arm rose, jerky and puppet-like, fingers twitching as white tendrils snaked beneath the torn cuffs of the rubber sleeve, anchoring the dead limb into the doorframe for balance. From deep inside the figure’s ruined throat came the same three measured, rhythmic clicks. One. Two. Three.
