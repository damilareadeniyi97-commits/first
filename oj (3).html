Elena pressed her back against the opposing bulkhead, her right hand instinctively closing around the cold aluminum shaft of her heavy inspection flashlight. Her breath came shallow and quiet through her teeth. The silence returned, dense and suffocating, punctuated only by the distant, rhythmic vibration of the idling engine. One. Two. Three. The three knocks hadn't been frantic. There was no frantic banging of fists, no muffled shouting from a survivor trapped in the cold belly of the ship. The impacts were deliberate, spaced precisely two seconds apart, striking the heavy steel with the mechanical consistency of a metronome. Elena stepped forward, her boots making no sound on the oil-stained diamond-tread floor. She raised the flashlight, sweeping its beam across the welded barricade. The rebar was structural grade, roughly four feet long, fused to the wheel and doorframe with multiple passes of arc weld. Near the hinges, an oxyacetylene cutting rig sat abandoned on a two-wheeled cart, its hoses coiled carelessly across the deck. The pressure gauges read near full, but the torch nozzle had been unscrewed and was nowhere to be seen. She crouched beside the door. At chest height, set into the reinforced bulkhead, was an eight-inch circular inspection port fitted with four-ply ballistic glass. The glass was completely obscured from the inside. Elena leaned in, bringing her flashlight flush against the glass to cut through internal reflections. A thick, greyish-white substance coated the interior surface, dried in strange, branching dendritic patterns like frost creeping across a winter windshield. It wasn't frost, though. The lower tunnel was stiflingly humid, the ambient air sitting at nearly ninety degrees. The crystalline crust had a chalky, fibrous texture that clung tenaciously to the viewport. Elena unclipped the portable multi-gas detector from her vest and held the probe up to the rubber seal running the perimeter of the door. The sensor clicked twice, then beeped in low, staggered intervals: Oxygen: 20.8% (Normal) Carbon Monoxide: 0 ppm Hydrogen Sulfide: 0 ppm LEL (Combustible Gases): 0% Volatile Organic Compounds: OFF SCALE HIGH The digital VOC readout flashed erratically before throwing a sensor error code: ERR: UNKNOWN ORGANIC MATRIX. A sharp scrape hissed from directly behind the door, like metal dragging over concrete, followed by the wet, suctioning sound of something pulling away from steel. Elena pulled back, her heart hammering against her ribs. She looked down at the Chief Engineer’s badge dangling from the wire. The plastic was bent, creased under heavy pressure, and the handwritten warning—DO NOT OPEN—had been etched deep enough to cut through the photo itself. She straightened up and followed the auxiliary wiring trunks running along the ceiling. A cluster of heavy black telemetry cables bypassed the standard ship conduit, routed haphazardly down the corridor toward an auxiliary electrical closet thirty feet back. She followed the cables. The door to the electrical closet was ajar, held open by a wedge of folded cardboard. Elena pushed it open. Inside, the wall-mounted junction boxes had been ripped open, their fuses replaced with heavy copper bypass bars. Mounted on an equipment rack bolted to the deck sat a portable mainframe unit—military-grade Pelican cases linked together with quick-connect bus cables, humming with internal cooling fans. A single ruggedized laptop rested on the rack, its screen bathed in an eerie monochrome blue. On the screen was a live, top-down schematic of the Orion Trader’s hull, tracking Hold 4. Instead of showing an empty container bay, the software mapped an intricate internal grid: thirty-six cryogenic containment modules, arranged in a hexagonal cluster around a central cooling spire. Thirty-five modules registered solid grey—offline or spent. The thirty-sixth module, positioned dead center at the lowest level against the keel, flashed a deep, pulsing crimson. Below the graphic, a system status line updated in real time: BIOLOGICAL ISOLATION STATUS: FAILING CONTAINMENT INTEGRITY: 14% PRIMARY SUBJECT: ACTIVE CORE BODY MASS INCREASE: +410% FEEDING CYCLE: STAGE 3 OF 4 Beneath the monitor, taped directly to the carbon-fiber chassis of the laptop, was a handwritten shipping manifest printout. The shipper was listed not as an automotive company or medical supplier, but as a shell entity registered out of Reykjavik: Vanguard Bio-Geophysical Consortium. And scribbled across the top of the manifest in the same blue ballpoint ink from the bridge logbook was a single, frantic note: It didn't drift off the shelf. We hauled it up from the trench. Should Elena dive into the files on this terminal to figure out what they hauled out of the trench, or head back to the main deck to secure an escape route before the storm hits?
