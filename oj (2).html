The vibration traveled through the soles of Elena’s boots before the sound reached her ears—a deep, metallic resonance that rattled the clipboards hanging against the bridge bulkhead. It wasn't the steady thrum of the diesel engine. It was an impact. Controlled, rhythmic, and originating from deep inside the hull. Elena unhooked the ship’s master manifest binder from the rack beneath the chart table. Her thumb flicked through the plastic sleeves, tracking the entries for Hold 4. Automotive parts. Industrial polymers. Medical equipment consigned to Montreal. Standard dry cargo. Nothing flagged for hazardous materials, pressurized storage, or temperature monitoring. Yet the thermal readout clutched in her hand still read: HOLD 4: PRESSURE EQUALIZATION COMPLETE. Dry containers didn't equalize pressure. Only vacuum-sealed transit units or specialized pressurized tanks did.

She moved to the central computer terminal and tapped the spacebar to wake the display. The screen flickered, showing the ship’s integrated automation system. The digital blueprint of the Orion Trader glowed green, except for a flashing amber quadrant near the keel.

Elena clicked into the surveillance sub-menu. The ship carried thirty-two closed-circuit cameras.

Cameras 1 through 6—Main Deck: empty containers, grey swell, rising mist.

Cameras 7 through 14—Superstructure corridors: vacant linoleum floors, closed cabin doors.

Camera 18—Engine Control Room: consoles illuminated, swivel chairs empty.

Cameras 21 through 24—Hold 4: NO SIGNAL.

Elena frowned, tapping the keyboard. The hold cameras hadn’t been severed by a power surge; their feeds had been deliberately rerouted to an internal loop. The timestamp on the error log showed the loop initiated twenty-eight hours ago, thirty minutes after the captain's final logbook entry. She pulled the ship’s handheld VHF from her tactical harness and pressed the push-to-talk button. "Pilot boat Gannet, this is Reyes. Do you read?" A heavy burst of white noise washed over the channel, laced with a faint, rhythmic clicking sound—like a mechanical relay opening and closing at rapid intervals. "Benny, do you copy?" she repeated.

The static broke for a half-second. A human voice whispered through the receiver, clipped and thin: "...not in the manifest..."
The audio cut completely. The radio emitted three flat beeps, and the digital display went dead. Elena popped the battery casing; the contacts were clean, the lithium pack fully seated, but the device refused to reboot. An electromagnetic dead zone, or deliberate localized jamming. A glance through the rain-streaked forward windows showed the ocean turning the color of slate. The squall was rolling in faster than forecasted, the horizon dissolving into curtains of dark squall-lines. Below on the main deck, the lashings between the container stacks groaned against the rising chop. She had an hour and a half before the pilot boat would be forced to pull back to open water. Leaving now meant filing an incomplete report, leaving an uncrewed, powered 600-foot ghost ship drifting straight toward the outbound transatlantic lanes. Elena racked her heavy flashlight into its holster, checked the air-quality tester clipped to her collar, and headed for the stairwell.

To reach Hold 4, she had to descend through the superstructure, cross the machinery spaces, and enter the lower pipe tunnel running beneath the container cells along the ship's spine. The air grew progressively warmer as she descended past the crew decks. By the time she reached the machinery flat above the auxiliary generators, the temperature hovered around ninety degrees. The smell changed too—the scent of diesel oil and sea salt giving way to a sharp, chemical tang, like ozone combined with damp chalk. She reached the watertight door marked ACCESS: HOLD 4 / PIPE TUNNEL LOWER. The heavy steel dogging wheels had been spun completely shut, but someone had welded a two-inch steel rebar straight across the locking mechanism, bridging the wheel to the frame. It was a crude, desperate weld—spatter marks scarred the yellow paint, cooling slag clinging to the metal. Someone hadn't tried to keep an intruder out.

They had welded the door shut from this side to make sure whatever was inside Hold 4 couldn't turn the wheel to get out. Beside the door, dangling from a stripped copper wire, hung a laminated crew badge. The photo showed the Chief Engineer, his face lined and sun-bleached. Scratched violently into the plastic coating with a knife tip were three words:
DO NOT OPEN.
From the other side of the six-inch steel bulkhead came a slow, measured knock.
One. Two. Three.
Then silence.
